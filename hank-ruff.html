Hank Ruff: Building His Own Path in Traditional Country

Southern Indiana country artist Hank Ruff talks about growing up around bluegrass, starting out with his dad's band, writing songs that sound true to him, and building a modern career without losing the steel guitar, fiddle, and direct storytelling that made him love country music.

About Hank Ruff

Hank Ruff is a country artist and songwriter from Bloomington, Indiana. He grew up around his father's bluegrass band, started playing mandolin when he was barely big enough to hold it, and later switched to guitar after deciding it might impress more girls in middle school.

Those bluegrass beginnings grew into a sound shaped by old-school country, the late 1980s and 1990s, and artists who keep traditional instrumentation in modern records. Hank now performs across the Midwest and beyond with his band, the Hellbenders, while continuing to release original music and build his audience independently.

From his dad's band to his own path

Hank's earliest musical education came from his dad, who plays mandolin and leads a band called the Dew Daddies. Hank grew up hearing Bill Monroe, Ralph Stanley, and old country records, then began playing seriously around fourth grade. By middle school he had moved toward guitar, but mandolin still appears in his live shows.

His first attempt at songwriting was unusually public. Hank wrote “The Prettiest Angel” for a middle-school crush and performed it at a choir concert in front of the school and parents. Years later, he placed the original Nashville acoustic demo at the end of his Hellbender EP rather than hiding where he started.

Hank still writes regularly with his dad and credits him with strong lyrical instincts. That family partnership connects his current music to the environment where he first learned to play, even as Hank has built a separate identity and touring band of his own.

Traditional country that still moves forward

Hank's influences stretch from the Carter Family, Jimmie Rodgers, Buck Owens, and Hank Williams to Dwight Yoakam, Clint Black, Travis Tritt, Brad Paisley, and Jon Pardi. The late-'80s and '90s records added a stronger backbeat and rock energy to the older country foundation he already knew.

He is not trying to recreate one past era or treat traditional country like a costume. His goal is to keep the elements that make the music unmistakably country—steel guitar, fiddle, strong melodies, and direct songwriting—while letting contemporary sounds appear naturally when the song calls for them.

Hank also talks about learning not to overcomplicate a lyric. Older country songs often said exactly what needed to be said. A clear line does not always need a clever twist added just to sound current.

Song stories from the episode

“The Prettiest Angel”

Hank wrote his first song in seventh grade to win back a girl and performed it at a school choir concert. The original acoustic recording eventually became the final track on the Hellbender EP, preserving the earliest point in his songwriting story.

“Whiskey Always Wins”

“Whiskey Always Wins” is an outside cut written by other songwriters. Hank recorded it because it felt close enough to his own experience and voice that he believed he could have written it himself. That believability is his standard for choosing somebody else's song.

“From Here”

The hook for “From Here” arrived after months of work. Following an Indiana University championship, Hank heard “Jack & Diane” on the ride home and connected “life goes on” with the question facing someone leaving college: where does it go from here? That phrase completed the song.

The Hellbenders, touring, and the work between shows

Live performance is the part of being an artist that keeps Hank most motivated. He began building his own audience while attending Indiana University, where he formed a band to replace the usual fraternity-party DJ with live country music. That path grew into shows around the Midwest, dates on both coasts, and a main-stage appearance at the Harley-Davidson Homecoming Festival with Jelly Roll, HARDY, Warren Zeiders, and Priscilla Block.

Hank named his band the Hellbenders after the tradition of country artists giving their touring groups their own identity. The name places them alongside the lineage of the Buckaroos, the Stray Horns, the Ace in the Hole Band, and the Strangers while also reflecting the musicians' tight-knit relationship on long van rides.

He typically plays 60 to 70 shows a year, enough to keep the live band sharp without eliminating time for writing, releases, and promotion. Hank handles his own social media and has seen consistent posting translate into audience growth and streams, even though it is not the part of the job he naturally enjoys most.
